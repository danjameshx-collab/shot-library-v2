# Shot Library Guide

A detailed breakdown of every shot in the Lincko shot library: what the shot is, what it is used for, why you would choose it, what it conveys, and how it moves the story, emotion or message forward.

Sections follow the library order. Shots tab first (Composition, then alphabetical), then the Sequences tab, then Styles.

Each entry covers:

- **The shot:** what it looks like and how it is built
- **Used for:** the moments and situations it fits
- **Why use it:** the job it does that other shots cannot
- **What it conveys:** the feeling or meaning the viewer takes from it
- **Storytelling role:** how it furthers the story, emotion or message

---

# Part 1: Shots

## Composition

### Frame In A Frame (Composition)

**The shot:** The subject is framed by something that already exists inside the scene: a doorway, a window, an arch, a car window, the gap between two shelves, a mirror edge, foliage. The outer frame is usually darker or out of focus, and the subject sits inside the brighter, sharper "window" within it. You end up with two layers of border: the edge of the screen and the edge of the object.

**Used for:** Introducing a character in their space, office and workshop scenes, quiet observational moments, establishing a subject inside a location without going fully wide, and scenes where you want the viewer to feel they are looking in on someone.

**Why use it:** It solves a common problem: busy locations pull the eye everywhere. The inner frame acts like a spotlight built out of the set itself, so the viewer knows exactly where to look without you needing lighting or a tighter lens. It also adds instant depth to an otherwise flat shot because there are now clear foreground and background planes.

**What it conveys:** Voyeurism and intimacy (we are peeking in), containment (the subject is boxed in by their world), or focus (this person matters, everything else is border). A tight, dark frame can feel trapped or pressured. A bright, open doorway can feel like opportunity or arrival.

**Storytelling role:** Frame in a frame tells the viewer something about the subject's relationship to their environment. A founder framed inside the doorway of a cramped office says "this is the box they are working in." The same founder framed by the doorway of a new building says "this is the threshold they are crossing." It is a quiet way to layer meaning into a shot without a single word of narration.

---

### Leading Lines (Composition)

**The shot:** Lines in the scene (a road, railing, corridor, fence, row of desks, shadows, cables, a pier, a shelf edge) run through the frame and converge on or point toward the subject. The eye follows the lines automatically and arrives at the person or object you want seen.

**Used for:** Walking shots, arrivals, establishing a destination, corridors and architectural spaces, product shots on long surfaces, and any static frame that needs a sense of movement.

**Why use it:** It is the most reliable way to direct attention in a still frame. Rather than asking the viewer to find the subject, the composition delivers them there. It also turns a flat 2D image into something with depth because converging lines read as distance.

**What it conveys:** Direction, purpose and inevitability. Lines that lead to a subject feel like a path or journey. Lines that lead past the subject toward the horizon feel like ambition or what is still ahead. Strong, straight lines feel structured and deliberate.

**Storytelling role:** Leading lines create visual momentum, which supports narrative momentum. Use them when the story is about progress, a journey, a goal, or a destination. A road running toward a figure in the distance tells the viewer "this is where we are heading" before anyone says it. They are also a strong way to transition attention from one part of the frame to another.

---

### Negative Space (Composition)

**The shot:** The subject sits small inside a large, mostly empty frame. Sky, a plain wall, open water, a blank studio, fog, a big empty room. The empty area is deliberate and often takes up two thirds or more of the picture.

**Used for:** Reflective beats, moments of loneliness or pressure, scale shots, minimalist product framing, and frames designed to hold on-screen text (the empty area is the perfect text space).

**Why use it:** Empty space gives the viewer room to feel something. It slows the pace of the edit and creates contrast after busy shots. Practically, it also gives you a clean canvas for titles and captions without covering anything important.

**What it conveys:** Isolation, solitude, vulnerability, calm, scale, or the weight of a decision. Depending on the context it can feel peaceful (a figure alone on a beach at sunrise) or heavy (a figure alone in an empty office late at night).

**Storytelling role:** Negative space is where the emotion lands. After a fast sequence, a negative space frame acts like a pause in a sentence: the audience digests what has just happened. It is ideal for "before" states in a transformation story (alone, stuck, overwhelmed) or for the quiet moment right before a turning point.

---

### Rule Of Thirds (Composition)

**The shot:** The frame is split into a 3x3 grid and the subject is placed on one of the lines or intersections rather than in the dead center. Eyes typically sit on the top third line, and the subject usually faces into the open side of the frame (look room).

**Used for:** Almost everything: interviews, talking heads, B-roll, landscapes, product shots. It is the default composition for natural-looking footage.

**Why use it:** Centered framing can feel static and formal. Off-center framing feels balanced, natural and more cinematic, and it leaves space in the frame for the environment, text or a second subject. It is the foundation that makes footage look intentional rather than accidental.

**What it conveys:** Naturalness, balance and comfort. Where the subject sits relative to their look room also carries meaning: facing into open space feels forward-looking and open; facing the near edge of the frame feels closed in, uneasy or backward-looking.

**Storytelling role:** Rule of thirds keeps the viewer relaxed and engaged so the content can do the work. It also lets you play with tension: flip a subject's look room against the norm and you subtly signal that something is off. In interviews, alternating left and right thirds between two speakers creates a visual conversation.

---

### Symmetry (Composition)

**The shot:** The frame is built around a central axis with matching elements on either side: a corridor, a centered doorway, a building facade, a desk with matching objects, a subject dead center in a balanced room.

**Used for:** Hero introductions, architectural and interior shots, brand moments, title shots, formal or authoritative scenes, and stylised "Wes Anderson" style beats.

**Why use it:** Symmetry breaks the natural rule of thirds pattern, which makes it stand out. It looks designed and controlled, which is ideal when you want a moment to feel important or premium.

**What it conveys:** Order, control, precision, calm, power and perfection. It can also feel slightly unnatural or uncanny, which is useful for tension or for showing a world that is a little too perfect.

**Storytelling role:** Use symmetry to mark key moments: the reveal of a founder at their desk, the first look at a finished product, the "after" state where everything is now in order. In a transformation story, moving from chaotic off-center framing to clean symmetry tells the viewer that order has been restored.

## Angle

### Ground (Angle)

**The shot:** The camera sits right on the ground, sometimes resting on the floor itself, looking up or across at the subject. Feet, wheels, objects and textures in the foreground loom large and the world stretches above.

**Used for:** Footsteps and arrivals, sports and action, product drops, skateboards, cars, animals, playful or energetic B-roll, and adding an unexpected angle to an otherwise standard sequence.

**Why use it:** Nobody sees the world from the floor, so it instantly feels fresh. It exaggerates movement and scale, makes small actions feel dramatic, and gives an edit a jolt of variety between eye-level shots.

**What it conveys:** Energy, immersion, rawness and grit. It puts the viewer "in the dirt" with the subject. Depending on use, it can feel playful, gritty or heroic.

**Storytelling role:** Ground shots signal the start of something physical: a step, an arrival, a journey beginning. They are a strong opener for an action sequence and a good way to show the effort side of a story (the grind, the work, the movement) rather than the polished result.

---

### High (Angle)

**The shot:** The camera sits above the subject and angles down toward them. Not straight overhead, just elevated, so we see the subject and a wider spread of the ground and space around them.

**Used for:** Context shots, showing a subject inside a busy space, vulnerable or reflective moments, desk and workspace shots, and crowd scenes.

**Why use it:** It opens up the environment and reveals layout and spatial relationships that eye level hides. It also gently shrinks the subject, which is useful when you want them to feel small against their circumstances.

**What it conveys:** Vulnerability, smallness, being watched, overwhelm, or simply an overview. In a softer context it can feel caring, like a parent looking down at a child.

**Storytelling role:** High angles work best for the "problem" part of a story: the founder buried in paperwork, the person who feels small against the challenge. Paired later with a low angle of the same subject, it creates a visual arc from powerless to powerful without a single word.

---

### Low (Angle)

**The shot:** The camera sits below eye level and tilts up at the subject. The subject towers in the frame, often with sky or ceiling behind them.

**Used for:** Hero shots, introductions of a leader or founder, moments of confidence and success, product hero shots, and authority beats in sales and brand videos.

**Why use it:** It is the fastest way to make a subject look bigger, stronger and more in control. It changes how the audience perceives status without changing anything about the person.

**What it conveys:** Power, authority, confidence, dominance and heroism. Pushed further it can feel intimidating or threatening.

**Storytelling role:** Use the low angle at the turning point and the payoff: when the subject takes control, solves the problem, or reaches the goal. In outreach and sales films it positions the subject (or the prospect) as the hero of the story, which is exactly how you want them to see themselves.

---

### Straight Up (Angle)

**The shot:** The camera points directly up at the sky or something towering overhead: skyscrapers, trees, a ceiling, a stadium roof, a person standing over the lens.

**Used for:** Establishing scale in cities or nature, opening a scene, transitions (sky to sky), moments of awe, and abstract graphic shots.

**Why use it:** It creates a dramatic sense of size that eye-level shots cannot. Buildings converge to a point, trees form a canopy, and the image becomes almost graphic. It is also a clean, neutral frame that cuts well between scenes.

**What it conveys:** Awe, ambition, scale, overwhelm and wonder. It makes the viewer feel small against something massive.

**Storytelling role:** Straight up shots set the stakes and the scale of a world: "this is the big city they are trying to conquer," "this is the giant company they are going up against." They work well as a breath between scenes and as a symbolic shot of aspiration (looking up to where you want to be).

---

### To The Side (Angle)

**The shot:** The camera is level with the subject and positioned to their side, capturing them in profile. Often used while the subject walks, works or looks at something, and often tracked alongside them.

**Used for:** Walking shots, working shots, contemplative moments, conversations between two people, and process B-roll.

**Why use it:** Profile framing removes the direct connection with the lens, so it feels observational and natural rather than performed. Tracking alongside gives a sense of travelling with the subject.

**What it conveys:** Companionship, observation, focus and thought. It feels like walking with someone rather than being spoken to by them. A held profile can also feel distant or introspective.

**Storytelling role:** Use it for the "journey" sections of a story, where the subject is in motion or deep in the work. It lets the audience sit beside the subject as a witness, which builds empathy without breaking the fourth wall.

---

### Top Down (Angle)

**The shot:** The camera looks straight down from directly above, flattening the scene into a two-dimensional, map-like composition. Desks, tables, food, hands, products, roads and courts all become graphic patterns.

**Used for:** Process shots (writing, cooking, building, unboxing), desk setups, product reveals, flat lays, drone overviews, and title backgrounds.

**Why use it:** It removes depth and turns real life into a clean graphic layout, which is perfect for showing a process clearly. Everything on the surface is visible at once, nothing hides behind anything else.

**What it conveys:** Clarity, organisation, control and a god's-eye view. It can also feel playful and design-led.

**Storytelling role:** Top down shots explain. They show how something is done, what is on the table, how the pieces fit together. In business stories they are ideal for showing the work behind the result: the plan being drawn, the notes being written, the product being assembled.

## Close Up

### Classic (Close Up)

**The shot:** Tight on the face (usually from the chin to just above the head) or on a single key detail, filling most of the frame with very little background.

**Used for:** Emotional lines in interviews, reactions, key statements, and any moment where the viewer must read the subject's face.

**Why use it:** It removes every distraction. The audience cannot look at anything else, so they read micro-expressions, eyes and emotion. It is where trust and connection are built.

**What it conveys:** Intimacy, honesty, intensity and vulnerability. There is nowhere to hide in a close up, so it feels truthful.

**Storytelling role:** Save close ups for the lines that matter most: the confession, the realisation, the promise. Cutting from a wide to a close up on a key line tells the viewer "listen to this." It is how you land the emotional core of a message.

---

### Extreme (Close Up)

**The shot:** Even tighter than a classic close up: just the eyes, a mouth, fingers on a keyboard, a pen nib on paper, the texture of fabric or skin. It can become almost abstract.

**Used for:** Punctuation beats, tension, detail inserts, intros, and transitions.

**Why use it:** Extreme close ups are jarring in a good way. They break the rhythm and force total focus on one tiny detail. Because they are almost abstract, they create intrigue: the viewer wants to know what they are looking at.

**What it conveys:** Intensity, tension, obsession, precision and significance. A tiny detail becomes the most important thing in the world.

**Storytelling role:** Use extreme close ups to punctuate a beat rather than sit in it: the eyes narrowing before a decision, the finger hovering over "send," the signature on a contract. They heighten drama right before a key moment and make small actions feel momentous.

---

### Fine Details (Close Up)

**The shot:** Macro-tight on the small details of a product or object: textures, stitching, buttons, grilles, knurled dials, engraved logos, the grain of wood. Usually shallow depth of field with slow, smooth movement.

**Used for:** Product videos, gear and tech features, premium brand shots, backgrounds for on-screen text, and intro sequences.

**Why use it:** Details sell quality. Showing the craftsmanship up close makes everyday objects feel premium and considered. The soft, textured frames also make excellent backdrops for titles because they are visually rich but not distracting.

**What it conveys:** Quality, craftsmanship, care, luxury and attention to detail.

**Storytelling role:** Fine details say "we sweat the small stuff." In a sales or brand film they transfer the idea of precision from the object to the person or company behind it. They are also a strong way to open a video slowly and build intrigue before revealing the full product.

---

### Foreground (Close Up)

**The shot:** A close, out-of-focus element sits in the foreground (a leaf, a glass, a shoulder, a doorframe, a plant) while the subject holds sharp focus behind it. The foreground blur partially covers the frame.

**Used for:** Interviews, intimate scenes, observational B-roll, and making a simple close up feel more cinematic.

**Why use it:** It adds depth and layers to a tight shot, and makes the viewer feel physically present in the scene, as if peering past something to see the subject. It also hides messy backgrounds.

**What it conveys:** Intimacy, discretion and presence. It can feel like a private moment we have stumbled onto.

**Storytelling role:** Foreground close ups make the audience feel like a participant, not a spectator. Use them for personal, honest moments where you want the viewer to feel close to the subject but slightly hidden, like they are being let in on something.

---

## Effect

### Low Shutter (Effect)

**The shot:** The shutter speed is set slower than normal (for example 1/15 or 1/8 instead of 1/50), so movement inside the frame smears into motion blur. Static elements stay sharp while moving people, lights or traffic streak.

**Used for:** Busy city scenes, nightlife, crowds, travel montages, dream sequences, chaotic or stressful moments, and energetic music-led edits.

**Why use it:** It turns ordinary movement into something stylised. It is a quick way to add energy and texture to footage, and it can make a scene feel either magical or overwhelming.

**What it conveys:** Chaos, speed, disorientation, euphoria or a dreamlike state. A subject sharp in the middle of a blurred crowd can feel isolated or calm in the storm.

**Storytelling role:** Low shutter visualises an internal state. Use it to show overwhelm (the founder lost in the noise of their business), the rush of a busy life, or a hazy memory. Contrast a low shutter chaos sequence with a crisp, steady shot afterward to show clarity arriving.

---

### Slow Motion (Effect)

**The shot:** Footage is captured at a high frame rate (60, 120 or 240fps) and played back at normal speed, stretching time. A one-second action becomes several seconds long.

**Used for:** Key moments, emotional beats, action highlights, product reveals, hair and fabric movement, liquids, and sports.

**Why use it:** Slow motion signals importance. It gives the audience time to take in a moment that would otherwise fly by, and it reveals beauty in movement the eye normally misses.

**What it conveys:** Significance, beauty, emotion, reflection and drama.

**Storytelling role:** Slow motion tells the viewer "this moment matters." Use it at the peak of an action, at the emotional high point, or for the reveal of a result. Use it sparingly; if everything is slow, nothing feels special.

---

### Speed Ramping (Effect)

**The shot:** A clip (or a run of clips) shifts smoothly between speeds mid-shot: from normal to slow motion, or from slow to fast, usually timed to a music beat or an action peak.

**Used for:** Action and sport, transitions, energetic montages, product reveals, travel edits, and hype pieces.

**Why use it:** It lets you have both energy and emphasis in a single shot. The speed-up drives pace and the slow-down highlights the key moment. Timed to music it makes an edit feel tight and polished.

**What it conveys:** Energy, impact, rhythm and control. It feels dynamic and modern.

**Storytelling role:** Speed ramps direct attention in time the way composition directs attention in space. They pull the viewer through the boring parts quickly and stop them exactly on the moment that matters: the jump, the handshake, the product landing on the table.

---

## Medium

### Behind (Medium)

**The shot:** The camera sits behind the subject at medium distance (roughly waist up), over or near their shoulder, showing what they are looking at: a screen, a view, a door, a crowd, a building.

**Used for:** Point-of-interest shots, arrivals, building anticipation, showing what a subject is working on, and reveals.

**Why use it:** It aligns the viewer with the subject's perspective without becoming a full POV. We see the person and what they see, which creates curiosity and identification.

**What it conveys:** Anticipation, curiosity, focus and shared perspective. It feels like standing right behind someone as they face something important.

**Storytelling role:** Use it just before a reveal or a challenge: the subject about to walk into a meeting, staring at a dashboard, looking at the city. It invites the audience to step into the subject's shoes and face the moment with them.

---

### Foreground (Medium)

**The shot:** A medium shot of the subject with something deliberately placed in the foreground: a plant, a lamp, a laptop edge, a coffee cup, a person's shoulder. The foreground can be soft or partially in focus.

**Used for:** Interviews, workplace B-roll, lifestyle scenes, and making medium shots feel richer and more cinematic.

**Why use it:** It adds layers and depth to a medium shot, which can otherwise feel flat. The foreground object can also carry meaning (a product, a tool, a personal item).

**What it conveys:** Being tucked into the world, presence, warmth and authenticity. It feels lived-in rather than staged.

**Storytelling role:** The foreground element can quietly add context: a camera in the foreground tells you the subject is a creator, a stack of invoices tells you they are buried in admin. It grounds the subject in their world and makes scenes feel authentic.

---

## Perspective

### Close Up Object (POV)

**The shot:** The camera takes the place of an object and is placed right up close to the action, framed tight: inside a fridge, at the bottom of a cup, inside a bag, on a keyboard, inside a mailbox. The subject interacts with it, so they look and reach toward the lens.

**Used for:** Playful product shots, everyday actions made interesting, social content, intros, and transitions.

**Why use it:** It is unexpected and immersive. It turns a simple action (opening a box, grabbing a drink) into a moment by putting the viewer inside the object at close range.

**What it conveys:** Intimacy, playfulness, curiosity and immediacy.

**Storytelling role:** Close up object POVs make the object a character. Use them when the object matters to the story: the package arriving, the product being opened, the phone being picked up. They make the viewer feel part of the action.

---

### Object (POV)

**The shot:** The camera becomes an object in the scene (a phone, a mirror, a laptop, a product, a dashboard) and the viewer sees exactly what that object would "see," usually at a slightly wider distance than the close up variant.

**Used for:** Showing interaction with tech, products and tools, playful perspective shots, and scenes about the relationship between a person and a thing.

**Why use it:** It flips the usual perspective and creates a fresh angle on routine scenes. Shooting from a laptop's view of the person typing, for example, makes a desk scene instantly more interesting.

**What it conveys:** Connection between person and object, curiosity, novelty, and sometimes surveillance or being watched.

**Storytelling role:** Object POVs tell the story of how the subject engages with the thing that matters: the tool they rely on, the product they love, the screen that dominates their day. In product and software stories, it puts the product at the center of the frame and the user's reaction in view.

---

### Reverse (POV)

**The shot:** The camera faces back at whoever is holding it, handheld, selfie style. The subject talks or reacts directly into the lens while moving through their world.

**Used for:** Vlogs, behind-the-scenes, walk-and-talks, social content, and raw, authentic moments.

**Why use it:** It is personal and direct. It feels unpolished in a good way, like the subject has picked up the camera to tell you something themselves.

**What it conveys:** Authenticity, intimacy, energy and immediacy. It feels like a FaceTime with a friend.

**Storytelling role:** Reverse POV builds trust and relatability. Use it when you want the message to feel personal and unproduced: a direct thank you, a behind-the-scenes update, a candid thought on the move. In outreach videos it makes the sender feel human and present.

## Talking Head

### High Angled (Talking Head)

**The shot:** Shot from a high angle looking down on the subject, who is sat on the ground or low with a mic. Graphic lines from the floor, architecture and hard shadows frame the subject and turn the conversation into a styled, designed composition.

**Used for:** Stylised interviews, creator content, podcasts with personality, and breaking up standard eye-level talking heads.

**Why use it:** It makes a plain conversation visually distinctive. The unusual angle plus strong graphic elements gives the talking head a designed, editorial feel.

**What it conveys:** Creativity, confidence, artistry and a relaxed, unconventional personality.

**Storytelling role:** Angled talking heads signal "this is not a standard interview." They tell the audience the creator or brand thinks differently, which supports any message about originality or doing things your own way.

---

### Classic (Talking Head)

**The shot:** The subject faces the camera directly (or just off-lens) at medium distance, roughly chest up, usually on the rule of thirds with a tidy, soft background.

**Used for:** Interviews, explainers, sales videos, testimonials, YouTube A-roll, and direct messages to camera.

**Why use it:** It is clear, reliable and easy to watch for long stretches. It keeps attention on the words and the person delivering them.

**What it conveys:** Directness, confidence, credibility and conversation. It feels like a face-to-face meeting.

**Storytelling role:** The classic talking head carries the argument. It is where the message is spoken plainly and where trust is earned. Everything else in the edit (B-roll, close ups, wides) supports what is said here.

---

### Composition (Talking Head)

**The shot:** The conversation is framed through foreground elements, such as trees or foliage, with the subjects small in the middle of the frame. The camera feels like it is observing from a distance.

**Used for:** Two-person conversations, outdoor interviews, documentary style pieces, and cinematic breaks within an interview.

**Why use it:** It turns a conversation into a scene. Instead of a direct address, the audience watches two people talk inside a beautiful environment, which feels more like a film than an interview.

**What it conveys:** Observation, intimacy at a distance, calm, and a sense of the subjects being part of something bigger.

**Storytelling role:** It lets the audience eavesdrop. Use it for reflective, candid conversations where the setting adds to the meaning, and as a breather between tighter, more intense talking head shots.

---

### Kitching Cuts (Talking Head)

**The shot:** The subject leans in off-center, framed in a lived-in room full of plants, props and warm practical lights. The edit cuts between tight, punchy angles (front, side, close, closer) to keep energy high while they talk.

**Used for:** YouTube content, educational videos, creator-style explainers, and fast-paced personal brand content.

**Why use it:** Long single-angle talking heads lose attention. Rapid cuts between angles reset the viewer's eye and maintain pace, while the warm room keeps it personal.

**What it conveys:** Energy, personality, warmth, authenticity and expertise delivered casually.

**Storytelling role:** Kitching cuts keep the viewer hooked through dense information. Each cut emphasises a new point or a punchline, creating a rhythm that makes the message easier to follow and harder to click away from.

---

### Landscape (Talking Head)

**The shot:** A seated talking head framed a little wider than usual, with a big open landscape stretching out behind: mountains, coast, fields, a city skyline.

**Used for:** Travel and lifestyle content, brand stories, reflective interviews, and pieces about vision or freedom.

**Why use it:** The landscape does the scene-setting while the subject talks. You get both A-roll and a sense of place in one shot, without needing to cut away.

**What it conveys:** Freedom, perspective, ambition, calm and vision.

**Storytelling role:** The backdrop becomes a metaphor. A founder talking with an open horizon behind them is literally and figuratively looking at the big picture. Use it for the "why" and "where we are going" parts of a story.

---

### Side Shot (Talking Head)

**The shot:** The camera sits off to the side, shooting past the edge of a monitor or desk object in the foreground. The subject talks just off-lens, so it feels like we are in the room watching them work.

**Used for:** Behind-the-scenes, work-in-progress content, tech and software stories, and second angles in interviews.

**Why use it:** It shows the subject in their working environment and adds a layered, documentary feel. It is also a perfect second angle to cut to from a classic talking head.

**What it conveys:** Authenticity, focus, work ethic and access. It feels like we have been allowed into the room.

**Storytelling role:** Side shots show the subject doing, not just saying. Use them to back up claims of expertise with visual proof of the work happening, and to give interviews a documentary credibility.

---

### Subject (Talking Head)

**The shot:** The subject owns the frame with big expressive gestures and props around them, against a dramatic backdrop. Personality comes first and place comes second.

**Used for:** Personality-led content, hooks, energetic explainers, and creator brands.

**Why use it:** It puts the energy and charisma of the speaker at the center. Big framing and expressive movement make the delivery the main attraction.

**What it conveys:** Confidence, charisma, passion and boldness.

**Storytelling role:** Use it when the person is the message. In personal brand and outreach content, it sells the human behind the offer and makes the viewer feel the energy they would get working with them.

---

### Unique (Talking Head)

**The shot:** Talking to camera from an unexpected spot: perched on the back of a truck, on a rooftop, in a workshop, on a boat, in a field. The chair is swapped for something that says more about who they are.

**Used for:** Personal brand intros, standout hooks, brand stories, and memorable openers.

**Why use it:** It is memorable. An unusual setting stops the scroll and communicates identity before the subject even speaks.

**What it conveys:** Personality, originality, lifestyle and authenticity.

**Storytelling role:** The location becomes part of the message. A farmer talking from the back of a truck or a builder talking on site instantly establishes credibility and character. It shows rather than tells who the subject is.

---

### Wide (Talking Head)

**The shot:** The subject sits small in the frame with an epic environment all around. A cinematic, breathing-room take on the talking head.

**Used for:** Opening statements, reflective moments, cinematic interviews, and brand films.

**Why use it:** It breaks the expectation of a tight talking head and gives the conversation a big, cinematic feel. The environment carries as much weight as the words.

**What it conveys:** Scale, calm, perspective and humility. The subject is part of a bigger world.

**Storytelling role:** Use the wide talking head to open or close a piece, or for big-picture statements. It frames the subject's words against the scale of their world, which gives the message weight.

---

## Unique

### Mirror (Unique)

**The shot:** The camera becomes a person's own eyes, often via a mirror or reflection: a bathroom mirror, a car mirror, a shop window, a puddle. The viewer sees the subject as they see themselves.

**Used for:** Morning routines, self-reflection moments, identity themes, transformation stories, and stylised B-roll.

**Why use it:** Reflections add an extra layer to the frame and naturally symbolise introspection. They also give you creative angles in tight spaces.

**What it conveys:** Self-reflection, identity, duality, honesty, and confronting yourself.

**Storytelling role:** Mirror shots mark moments of reckoning: the subject looking at who they are, who they have become, or who they want to be. They are powerful at the start of a transformation and again at the end.

---

### Shadows (Unique)

**The shot:** Shadows lead the eye, partially hide the subject, or become the subject itself: silhouettes on a wall, window blinds striping a face, a figure's shadow stretching across the ground.

**Used for:** Moody intros, mystery, stylised B-roll, and dramatic or emotional moments.

**Why use it:** Shadows add mood and drama without extra lighting gear, just by using hard light from the sun or a window. They create shape and contrast in otherwise simple scenes.

**What it conveys:** Mystery, tension, drama, introspection and intrigue. They hint at what is hidden.

**Storytelling role:** Shadows hold back information. Use them to build intrigue before a reveal (show the shadow before the person), to represent doubt or a darker period, or to give a scene a cinematic, noir quality.

---

## Wide

### AWTA (Wide)

**The shot:** A Walk-Towards or Walk-Away shot at wide distance. The subject moves through a big environment toward or away from the camera.

**Used for:** Scene openers and closers, arrivals and departures, journeys, and transitions between locations.

**Why use it:** It is a classic, reliable shot that combines subject, movement and location in one frame. It sets the scene while showing action.

**What it conveys:** Walking towards: arrival, purpose, confidence and approach. Walking away: departure, closure, moving on, or heading into the unknown.

**Storytelling role:** AWTA marks the beginning and end of chapters. A walk toward camera opens a scene with intent; a walk away closes it with finality. Together they bracket a story neatly.

---

### Behind (Wide)

**The shot:** A wide shot from behind the subject, showing the full environment they are heading into or looking at.

**Used for:** Arrivals, reveals, adventure and travel, and showing the challenge ahead.

**Why use it:** It sets the scale of the world before the subject engages with it, and lets the viewer see what the subject sees.

**What it conveys:** Anticipation, scale, the unknown, and a challenge ahead.

**Storytelling role:** Use it before a big moment: the subject facing the conference hall, the mountain, the new city, the empty warehouse. It frames the challenge and invites the audience to wonder what comes next.

---

### Classic (Wide)

**The shot:** The standard wide establishing shot: full body, full environment, no tricks.

**Used for:** Establishing scenes, giving context, coverage, and resetting the viewer's sense of space.

**Why use it:** Every scene needs one. Without it the viewer does not know where they are or how the pieces fit together.

**What it conveys:** Context, clarity and orientation.

**Storytelling role:** The classic wide answers "where are we?" It grounds the scene so that close ups and details make sense, and it is the safe anchor to cut back to whenever the edit needs to reset.

---

### Extreme (Wide)

**The shot:** The subject is small against a huge environment: a figure on a mountain, a car on an empty road, a person on a vast beach.

**Used for:** Openers, scale shots, travel, nature, and dramatic emphasis.

**Why use it:** It establishes location and stakes in a single frame and creates a striking, cinematic image.

**What it conveys:** Scale, isolation, adventure, awe and insignificance or ambition, depending on context.

**Storytelling role:** Extreme wides set the size of the challenge or the world. They are perfect at the very start of a film to establish ambition, or at the end to show how far the subject has come.

---

### Foreground (Wide)

**The shot:** A wide shot layered with a foreground element (branches, rocks, a fence, a window frame) for depth.

**Used for:** Establishing shots, cinematic scene-setting, and landscape and location shots.

**Why use it:** It stops wides feeling flat by adding depth layers. It looks considered and cinematic.

**What it conveys:** Depth, presence, and the feeling of watching from within the world.

**Storytelling role:** It places the viewer inside the environment rather than looking at it from outside, which makes scene-setting feel immersive and intentional.

---

### Head On (Wide)

**The shot:** The subject faces the camera directly from a wide distance, with the environment fully visible around them.

**Used for:** Hero shots, confident introductions, team shots, and closing images.

**Why use it:** It combines the directness of eye contact with the context of a wide. The subject confronts the viewer while owning their space.

**What it conveys:** Confidence, groundedness, presence and readiness.

**Storytelling role:** Use it for the "this is who we are" moment: the founder standing in their warehouse, the team in their office. It is a statement shot that says the subject is ready.

---

### Low (Wide)

**The shot:** A wide shot from a low camera position, looking up across the scene.

**Used for:** Heroic establishing shots, architecture, events, and sports.

**Why use it:** It adds power and scale to the whole environment, not just the subject.

**What it conveys:** Grandeur, power, ambition and importance.

**Storytelling role:** Low wides make a world feel epic. Use them to elevate a location or event, so the audience feels the stakes and significance of where the story is happening.

---

## Stylised Shots

### Actions From Angles (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A small everyday action (a hand picking up a phone off the sofa, keys being grabbed, a door opening) is filmed from an unusual, tight angle, with texture and light doing the work.

**Used for:** B-roll, intros, transitions, and lifestyle content.

**Why use it:** It turns throwaway moments into cinematic ones and adds visual interest to otherwise mundane parts of a story.

**What it conveys:** Craft, intentionality, and the beauty of everyday life.

**Storytelling role:** It shows the routine of a character in a way that feels considered. Use it to build a character's world through small actions, or to signal the start of something (picking up the phone = a decision made).

---

### APS-C (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A moody, tightly cropped talking-head clip shot on an APS-C sensor for extra reach, giving a punchier, more compressed look.

**Used for:** Hooks, testimonials, punchy statements, and moody interview cutaways.

**Why use it:** The crop and compression give a distinct, intimate look that contrasts with full-frame wides. It feels close and intense.

**What it conveys:** Intensity, intimacy, urgency and mood.

**Storytelling role:** Use it for the lines you want to hit hardest. Cutting to a tight, compressed angle for a hook or key testimonial line raises the emotional temperature instantly.

---

### Before Context Sequence (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** Open on a small, intimate detail (a hand wrapping around a mug, a pen on a page) before showing where you are.

**Used for:** Scene openers, intros, and building intrigue.

**Why use it:** It hooks the viewer with curiosity. They lean in first and get the context after.

**What it conveys:** Intimacy, curiosity, and slow-building tension.

**Storytelling role:** It reverses the usual establish-first pattern, which makes the audience active: they piece the scene together. It is great for openers that need to grab attention without giving everything away.

---

### Close Up & Context (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A tight close up of a detail (like a birthday banner) paired with a wider shot that shows the people and the room around it.

**Used for:** Events, celebrations, lifestyle scenes, and storytelling through objects.

**Why use it:** The close up grabs attention, and the wide shot explains it. It is a simple, effective two-beat storytelling unit.

**What it conveys:** Detail and meaning followed by understanding.

**Storytelling role:** It teaches the viewer to look at details and then rewards them with context. Use it to make a scene feel layered and to let an object hint at what is happening before you show it.

---

### Close Up Sequence (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A run of tight close-up shots cut back to back, never pulling back to breathe.

**Used for:** Intense moments, process sequences, emotional peaks, and product montages.

**Why use it:** It stacks emotional or visual beats and keeps the intensity high.

**What it conveys:** Intensity, focus, pressure and immersion.

**Storytelling role:** Use it when you want the audience locked in: the build-up to a launch, the stress of a deadline, the craft of making something. Releasing into a wide afterward feels like a breath of relief.

---

### Cut Sequence Zoom (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A run of cutaway inserts that punch progressively closer on the same action, shot by shot, before the payoff.

**Used for:** Build-ups, reveals, dramatic emphasis, and comedic beats.

**Why use it:** It creates rising tension and anticipation with every cut.

**What it conveys:** Building tension, focus and inevitability.

**Storytelling role:** It is a visual drumroll. Use it right before a payoff (the reveal, the punchline, the key result) so the audience is primed for impact.

---

### Duplication (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** The same subject appears multiple times within one frame, cloned across the scene doing different things.

**Used for:** Creative content, showing multitasking, playful brand videos, and explaining many roles.

**Why use it:** It is eye-catching and communicates the idea of "doing everything at once" visually.

**What it conveys:** Busyness, multitasking, overwhelm, playfulness or capability.

**Storytelling role:** Perfect for the "wearing too many hats" problem: the founder who is the salesperson, the accountant and the support desk at once. It visualises the pain point that automation or delegation solves.

---

### Ending Context Wide (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** The scene closes on a wide of the location (like a lit-up diner at night) so the viewer finally sees the whole place.

**Used for:** Scene closers, video endings, and emotional resolutions.

**Why use it:** It gives a calm, full-stop ending that ties every earlier detail together.

**What it conveys:** Resolution, completeness and reflection.

**Storytelling role:** It is the period at the end of the sentence. After a sequence of details, the wide brings everything into one picture and lets the story settle.

---

### Environment (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A medium-distance frame that keeps enough of the location visible to set the scene, balancing subject and place evenly.

**Used for:** Scene-setting, lifestyle content, and showing a subject in their element.

**Why use it:** It shows both who and where without favouring either.

**What it conveys:** Belonging, context and balance.

**Storytelling role:** It establishes the subject as part of their world. Use it to show the subject at home in their environment, which supports any story about identity, craft or place.

---

### Match Cuts (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** Shots linked by matching action, shape or motion across the cut, so one scene flows straight into the next without a visible seam.

**Used for:** Transitions, montages, time jumps, and showing parallels.

**Why use it:** It makes an edit feel seamless and clever, and connects different moments visually.

**What it conveys:** Continuity, connection, transformation and the passage of time.

**Storytelling role:** Match cuts link ideas. A hand closing a laptop in one place matched to a hand opening a door somewhere else connects two moments into one story. Use them to show time passing or to draw parallels between before and after.

---

### POV (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A first-person, often fisheye action-cam angle that puts the viewer directly in the subject's hands or eyeline.

**Used for:** Action, sports, hands-on work, adventure, and social content.

**Why use it:** It puts the viewer in the driver's seat for a raw, in-the-moment experience.

**What it conveys:** Immersion, energy, immediacy and adrenaline.

**Storytelling role:** It turns the viewer into the protagonist. Use it to make the audience feel what the subject feels: the rush, the work, the experience.

---

### Props (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A shot built around a prop, letting the object anchor the frame and carry the story.

**Used for:** Symbolic storytelling, product shots, physical goods (lumpy mail, gifts), and character-building.

**Why use it:** Props carry meaning efficiently. A single object can communicate a theme, a backstory or a message.

**What it conveys:** Symbolism, meaning and personality.

**Storytelling role:** Props act as visual metaphors and story anchors. In outreach and Super Loom work, a carefully chosen object can carry the entire concept: it is the thing the viewer remembers.

---

### Reveal (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** The frame starts hidden or obscured, then moves or opens to reveal the subject at medium distance.

**Used for:** Intros, product reveals, character introductions, and payoffs.

**Why use it:** It builds a payoff inside a single shot, rewarding curiosity.

**What it conveys:** Surprise, anticipation and discovery.

**Storytelling role:** Reveals turn an introduction into an event. Use them for the first look at a key person, product or place so it lands with impact.

---

### Slow Motion Sequence (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A run of shots all captured at a high frame rate and played back slow.

**Used for:** Emotional montages, celebrations, brand films, and climaxes.

**Why use it:** It stretches a string of small moments into something significant and gives the edit a dreamy, heightened feel.

**What it conveys:** Emotion, nostalgia, significance and beauty.

**Storytelling role:** Use it for the emotional climax or the "what it all means" montage. It slows the audience down so they feel the weight of the moment.

---

### Top-Down (Stylised Shots)

**The shot:** A sequence built entirely from overhead angles, flattening every beat into a graphic, process-driven feel.

**Used for:** Process explanations, cooking, crafting, desk work, planning, and product assembly.

**Why use it:** It makes processes clear, organised and visually satisfying.

**What it conveys:** Order, method, craftsmanship and clarity.

**Storytelling role:** It explains how something is done step by step. Use it to show the system or method behind a result, which builds credibility.

---

# Part 2: Sequences

## Full Coverage

### 9 Shot Rule Classic (Full Coverage)

**The shot:** A coverage formula for any scene: 2 drone, 2 wide, 2 medium, 2 close up, 1 unique, shot and cut in that order. The edit moves from the biggest view to the smallest, then finishes on something unexpected.

**Used for:** Locations, events, venues, product launches, travel scenes, and any time you need to cover a scene fully and quickly without overthinking it.

**Why use it:** It guarantees you never leave a location without what you need. The order mirrors how people naturally take in a new place (big picture, then people, then details), so the edit feels effortless to follow.

**What it conveys:** Clarity, completeness and a confident, guided tour of a place.

**Storytelling role:** It establishes the world, introduces the people in it, then pulls the viewer into the details that matter, with the unique shot as the signature moment the audience remembers. It is the dependable backbone of a scene that every other technique can build on.

---

### 9 Shot Rule Mixed (Full Coverage)

**The shot:** The same 9 shots (drone, wide, medium, close up, unique) captured as normal but intercut out of order in the edit, so the viewer jumps between scales.

**Used for:** Energetic montages, events, music-led edits, social content and highlight reels.

**Why use it:** The classic order is predictable. Mixing the scales keeps the eye working and gives the sequence rhythm and surprise while still delivering full coverage.

**What it conveys:** Energy, excitement, movement and a sense that a lot is happening at once.

**Storytelling role:** It captures the feeling of being there rather than the logic of the space. Use it when the emotion of a scene matters more than its geography, such as the buzz of a launch day or the chaos of a busy business.

---

### 9 Shot Rule Reverse (Full Coverage)

**The shot:** The 9-shot coverage run back to front: close up and unique first, then medium and wide, finishing on drone.

**Used for:** Openers, reveals, endings and pieces built around mystery or a payoff.

**Why use it:** Starting on details creates curiosity. The viewer does not know where they are, so each wider shot adds information until the drone shot reveals the full picture.

**What it conveys:** Intrigue, discovery, and a growing sense of scale.

**Storytelling role:** It builds outward from the personal to the epic, which suits stories about something small growing into something big, or ending a film on a grand, zoomed-out "look how far we have come" image.

---

## Camera

### Lots Of Cuts (Camera)

**The shot:** Rapid cuts between many short angles of the same moment, often shot through foreground gear and people. Each clip lasts a second or less, and the angles change in height, distance and direction.

**Used for:** Events, busy workplaces, launches, behind-the-scenes, and hype pieces.

**Why use it:** The pace makes an event feel busy, packed and alive. Even a modest moment looks bigger when it is covered from many angles in quick succession.

**What it conveys:** Energy, activity, momentum, demand and excitement.

**Storytelling role:** It turns a single moment into an experience. Use it to show that the business, event or team is thriving and in demand, or to build energy right before a calm, focused beat so the contrast lands.

---

## Drone

### Drone Sequence (Drone)

**The shot:** A run of aerial shots cut together (reveals, orbits, top downs, push-ins, fly-overs) to establish scale and location before dropping into ground-level coverage.

**Used for:** Openers, locations, property, travel, events and brand films.

**Why use it:** Nothing establishes place and scale faster. It also gives a production an immediate high-end feel.

**What it conveys:** Scale, ambition, freedom and production value.

**Storytelling role:** Drone sequences answer "where are we and how big is this" in seconds, which sets the stakes for the story. Dropping from the sky to ground level also works as a natural transition from the big picture into the personal story.

---

## Introductions

### Cinematics & Talking Head (Introductions)

**The shot:** Open on crafted cinematic shots (like a styled top down of a notebook titled with the topic), then cut to the talking head.

**Used for:** YouTube intros, explainer videos, brand films and outreach videos.

**Why use it:** The visuals frame the subject and topic before the host takes over, raising perceived quality from the first second.

**What it conveys:** Professionalism, craft and intentionality.

**Storytelling role:** The cinematic opener sets the tone and theme, then the talking head delivers the message. It tells the audience "this is worth your time" before a single word is spoken.

---

### Cinematics & Voiceover (Introductions)

**The shot:** Open on slow, moody cinematic shots (like hands on a passport in warm window light) carried by a voiceover instead of a face on camera.

**Used for:** Story-driven intros, documentaries, emotional brand films and Super Looms.

**Why use it:** Holding back the face builds intrigue. The voice gives the images meaning, and the viewer wants to know who is speaking.

**What it conveys:** Mystery, emotion, depth and a sense of story.

**Storytelling role:** It sets up the story before revealing the storyteller. Use it when the narrative matters more than the presenter, or when the reveal of who is talking is part of the payoff.

---

### Music Montage Into A-Roll (Introductions)

**The shot:** Open on a music-led montage of the scene and the people in it, then land on the first line of A-roll.

**Used for:** Vlogs, event recaps, brand stories and lifestyle content.

**Why use it:** It sets the mood and the world before anyone speaks, so the first line lands with context already in place.

**What it conveys:** Energy, atmosphere and anticipation.

**Storytelling role:** The montage builds the world and the A-roll starts the story inside it. The switch from music to voice is a clear "we are starting now" signal.

---

### Straight Into Raw (Introductions)

**The shot:** No setup, no titles: the video opens mid-moment on raw handheld footage with the subject already talking.

**Used for:** Vlogs, social content, behind-the-scenes and authentic personal brand videos.

**Why use it:** It is the fastest hook there is. The viewer feels like they have walked in on something real.

**What it conveys:** Authenticity, urgency and immediacy.

**Storytelling role:** It skips the warm-up and goes straight to the human moment, earning attention through realness instead of polish.

---

### Talking Head & B-Roll (Introductions)

**The shot:** Open straight on a close talking head speaking to camera with bold words popping on screen, then cut into B-roll as the story builds.

**Used for:** YouTube, educational content, sales videos and social clips.

**Why use it:** A face plus a strong line is the most reliable hook. The text reinforces the message for silent viewers and the B-roll keeps the visuals moving.

**What it conveys:** Directness, clarity and confidence.

**Storytelling role:** It states the promise up front, then illustrates it. The viewer knows immediately what the video is about and why they should stay.

---

## Transitions

### Music (Transitions)

**The shot:** A music cue carries the change of scene, like a beat drop landing on a clean shot of handwritten notes.

**Used for:** Chapter changes, mood shifts and montages.

**Why use it:** A change in sound signals a change in scene without any visual device, which keeps the edit clean.

**What it conveys:** A shift in mood, energy or time.

**Storytelling role:** The new sound tells the viewer "we have moved on" and sets the emotional tone of the next section before it begins.

---

### Object Gives Context (Transitions)

**The shot:** Cut to a low, close shot of a telling object (like skates being set down on the floor) before the next scene.

**Used for:** Changes of location or activity, and jumps in the story.

**Why use it:** The object tells the viewer where the story is going before they see it, which makes the transition feel smooth and clever.

**What it conveys:** Anticipation and continuity.

**Storytelling role:** It bridges two scenes with meaning instead of a blank cut, and lets the audience predict the next beat, which keeps them engaged.

---

### Speech Adds Context (Transitions)

**The shot:** The subject watches or listens to something (like a speech playing on a screen) and its audio carries us into the next scene.

**Used for:** Thematic transitions, documentaries and narrative pieces.

**Why use it:** The words the subject hears set up what comes next, so the transition delivers information as well as movement.

**What it conveys:** Connection, theme and inspiration.

**Storytelling role:** It links scenes through ideas rather than visuals, and lets another voice introduce the next chapter.

---

### Text Card (Transitions)

**The shot:** Cut to a black frame with a small, quiet serif card: the chapter number in italics and a one-line title beneath.

**Used for:** Chapter breaks in longer films, documentaries and Super Looms.

**Why use it:** It gives the viewer a breath and a clear sense of structure.

**What it conveys:** Structure, calm, and a literary, considered tone.

**Storytelling role:** Text cards split the story into chapters, signal a new part, and frame what comes next with a single line.

---

### Text Card With A Roll (Transitions)

**The shot:** The same quiet serif text card, laid over footage instead of black, sitting in the empty side of the frame while the scene plays.

**Used for:** Chapter breaks that need to keep momentum.

**Why use it:** It marks a new chapter without stopping the story or killing the pace.

**What it conveys:** Structure combined with continuity.

**Storytelling role:** It labels the new section while the visuals keep moving, so the viewer gets their bearings without a pause.

---

# Part 3: Styles

### Black & White

**The shot:** The whole grade drops to high-contrast black and white, with deep shadows and hard window light.

**Used for:** Documentary moments, flashbacks, emotional sections and premium brand pieces.

**Why use it:** It strips out colour distractions and focuses attention on mood, texture, faces and light.

**What it conveys:** Timelessness, seriousness, nostalgia and honesty.

**Storytelling role:** Black and white can separate time periods (past versus present), mark an emotional section, or give the whole piece a classic documentary weight.

---

### Title Card

**The shot:** Huge bold words scattered across the frame over black and white footage, with the subject framed in the gap between them.

**Used for:** Video openers, chapter openers and brand statements.

**Why use it:** It opens a video like a film poster, setting the title and the tone in one striking image.

**What it conveys:** Boldness, confidence and cinematic ambition.

**Storytelling role:** It announces the story. The words and the subject together tell the viewer what this film is about, and who it is about, before it begins.
